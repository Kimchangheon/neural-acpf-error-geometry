\begin{abstract}
Recent neural power-flow solvers, including emerging foundation models,
achieve accurate voltage predictions, yet such accuracy does not
necessarily imply physically consistent solutions.
Even small voltage errors can yield large AC power-balance residuals,
particularly on large-scale grids.
We study this accuracy--consistency gap across PIGNN, GridSFM, GridFM-GraphKit, and LUMINA on realistic 2224-bus GBnetwork scenarios, with cross-grid evaluation of GridSFM over 31 systems.
Using an SVD basis fitted to training AC power-flow solutions, we find that neural prediction errors contain substantial components outside the dominant solution subspace.
To address this mismatch,
calibrated solution-subspace projection (CSP) removes off-subspace prediction components,
substantially reducing mean power-balance residual from 0.3159 to 0.0550 p.u. for PIGNN-GC and
from 0.2687 to 0.0514 p.u. for GridSFM.
These results highlight output-error geometry as a critical factor in physics-consistent neural AC power flow.
\end{abstract}

AC power flow, neural surrogate, physics violation, error geometry, calibration, distribution shift

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Modern power-system operation increasingly requires evaluating large ensembles of
operating conditions. Variable renewable generation, electrification, storage, and
spatially concentrated loads increase the variability of power injections and demand
\cite{decarne2024storage}, while finite transmission capacity makes congestion,
curtailment, and corrective redispatch increasingly consequential
\cite{titz2024congestion,newbery2024curtailment}.
In Germany, Redispatch~2.0 uses load-flow-based grid-security analysis to anticipate
bottlenecks and coordinate corrective actions across transmission and distribution
systems \cite{belz2024redispatch}. Related studies also highlight the growing
computational demands placed on load-flow analysis under increasingly diverse operating
conditions \cite{conrad2025data}.
In nodal electricity markets, transmission constraints shape locational prices and
therefore affect both operational and commercial decisions
\cite{weibelzahl2017nodal}.
AC power flow (AC-PF) is consequently a fundamental nonlinear computational
primitive, mapping injections and network conditions to bus voltages and
network flows needed for system assessment \cite{karimi2019loadflow}.
Repeated AC-PF evaluations arise in time-series and contingency studies
\cite{oz2026dpf}, while the same nonlinear AC network equations also underlie
optimal power flow and state estimation \cite{monticelli2000state}, where they define operational
constraints and nonlinear measurement mappings, respectively.
\cite{pacaud2027scopf}.

The computational demands of repeated grid analysis have motivated learned
surrogates that amortize the cost of conventional solvers over many operating
points \cite{lin2024powerflownet}.
Learned grid solvers span different levels of constraint enforcement:
direct regression prioritizes fast inference \cite{arowolo2026hhmpnn},
physics- or constraint-aware objectives penalize violations during training
\cite{piloto2024canos,wen2026pigat},
while iterative correction and feasibility-seeking mechanisms refine
predictions more directly toward physically consistent or feasible states
\cite{kim2026pignn,nguyen2025fsnet}.
More recent cross-grid and foundation-model efforts---including GridSFM
\cite{yang2026gridsfm}, GridFM-GraphKit and GENCO
\cite{gridfmgraphkit2026,puech2026genco}, and LUMINA
\cite{li2026lumina}---extend this direction with global context and rich
heterogeneous, topology-aware representations designed to operate across
diverse grids and operating conditions.

Despite this progress, physical consistency remains distinct from state
prediction accuracy. Learning-based AC-PF methods commonly regress toward
reference solutions obtained from numerical power-flow solvers
\cite{lin2024powerflownet}, while physics-informed variants additionally
penalize AC power-balance residuals during training \cite{wen2026pigat}.
Some formulations go further and optimize the governing residuals directly
without reference states \cite{kim2026pignn,jiang2025upinn}.
Modern evaluations accordingly report both voltage-state errors and reconstructed
AC power-balance residuals \cite{wen2026pigat,puech2026genco}.
However, incorporating residuals into the objective does not enforce the AC
equations as hard constraints: non-negligible power-balance violations can remain
even when state errors are small and residuals are explicitly minimized.
The central question is therefore why such inconsistency persists after both
accurate state fitting and physics-aware training.
We argue that the missing factor lies in the geometry of the remaining voltage-error pattern across buses.

We investigate this \emph{accuracy--consistency gap} across PIGNN, GridSFM,
GridFM-GraphKit, and LUMINA using realistic 2224-bus GBnetwork scenarios and
cross-grid evaluation over 31 systems of diverse sizes.
Detailed analysis of PIGNN-GC and GridSFM first shows that train-only calibration
substantially improves voltage accuracy while leaving non-negligible AC
power-balance residuals, indicating that persistent bias alone cannot explain the gap.
To characterize the remaining error, we construct an SVD basis exclusively from
training AC power-flow solutions and examine how voltage-error patterns align with
the resulting training-supported solution subspace.
While related PCA-based subspaces have previously been used to compress AC-OPF
learning outputs \cite{park2024compact}, we instead use the subspace as a coordinate
system for diagnosing voltage-prediction error relative to training-supported solution
variation.
We find that substantial error can extend outside this dominant solution subspace,
and that the off-subspace component is strongly associated with AC power-balance
violation.
Motivated by this finding, we introduce \emph{calibrated solution-subspace
projection} (CSP), which first removes train-estimated bias and then removes
prediction components outside the dominant training-supported solution directions.
CSP improves voltage accuracy and AC power balance simultaneously, while controlled
distribution shifts show that the useful solution geometry is low-dimensional yet
conditioned on the operating distribution.
Together, these results identify voltage-error geometry across buses as a key link
between state accuracy and physical consistency.

\section{Error Geometry and Calibrated Subspace Projection}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{AC consistency and train-only calibration}

We represent the grid as a directed graph
$\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})$ with $N=|\mathcal{V}|$ buses.
Each branch follows the standard $\pi$-equivalent model with transformer
tap ratios and phase shifts, yielding direction-dependent branch admittances
and an asymmetric bus-admittance matrix
$\underline{Y}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}$.
The voltage state is
$x=[V,\,\theta]\in\mathbb{R}^{2N}$, where
$V=|\underline{V}|$ and
$\underline{V}=V\odot e^{\jmath\theta}$.
Given an operating condition $u$, comprising nodal setpoints and
$\pi$-model branch parameters, a neural surrogate predicts
$\hat{x}=f_\phi(u)$.
Currents are $\underline{I}=\underline{Y}\underline{V}$ and complex powers are
$\underline{S}=\underline{V}\odot\underline{I}^*$, giving
$P=\Re\{\underline{S}\}$ and $Q=\Im\{\underline{S}\}$.
For setpoints $(P^{\mathrm{set}},Q^{\mathrm{set}})$, define residuals
\[
\Delta P=P^{\mathrm{set}}-P,
\qquad
\Delta Q=Q^{\mathrm{set}}-Q.
\]
The AC power-flow residual and corresponding nodal power-balance magnitude are
\[
r(\hat{x};u)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\Delta P\\
\Delta Q
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\mathrm{Power~Balance~(PB)}
=
\sqrt{\Delta P^2+\Delta Q^2}.
\]
PB is evaluated bus-wise using the standard slack/PV/PQ conventions,
and its mean and maximum over buses and scenarios are reported as
Mean PB and Max PB.
Accordingly, we define AC consistency as satisfaction of the AC power-balance
equations, quantified by PB.
All residuals are recomputed from the final predicted voltage state.

Before analyzing error geometry, we apply a train-only calibration that removes
post-training bias.
For training scenario $s$, let $\hat{x}_s$ and $x_s^\star$ denote the model
prediction and reference solution, respectively.
The bias is estimated after training and frozen for unseen test scenarios:
\[
    \hat b
    =
    \mathbb{E}_{s\in\mathrm{tr}}
    \left[\hat x_s-x^\star_s\right],
    \qquad
    \hat x^C=\hat x-\hat b.
\]
For phase angles, offsets are estimated using circular differences after
consistent gauge alignment.
This calibration corrects persistent output bias but does not constrain the
directions in which the remaining error varies across buses.
\subsection{Error geometry in a training-derived solution subspace}
To characterize the remaining error, we construct a low-dimensional basis
from Newton--Raphson solutions in the training set.
Let $\bar x_{\mathrm{tr}}$ denote the training-solution mean and define
\begin{equation}
X=
\begin{bmatrix}
x_1^\star-\bar x_{\mathrm{tr}} & \cdots &
x_{N_{\mathrm{tr}}}^\star-\bar x_{\mathrm{tr}}
\end{bmatrix}
=
W\Sigma U^\top,\;
P_k=W_kW_k^\top
\end{equation}
where $P_k$ denotes the block-diagonal projection onto the dominant rank-$k$
magnitude and gauge-aligned angle subspaces identified from the training data.
These subspaces capture dominant solution variation under the training
distribution and are therefore distribution dependent.
We use them to characterize the geometry of the model's prediction error.
For $e=\hat x-x^\star$, we decompose
\begin{equation}
    e^{\parallel}=P_k e,
    \qquad
    e^{\perp}=(I-P_k)e ,
    \label{eq:error_decomposition}
\end{equation}
where $e^{\parallel}$ lies along the dominant training-solution directions
and $e^{\perp}$ captures error outside them.

%This decomposition is relevant to physical consistency because the AC
%residual is locally sensitive not only to the magnitude of the voltage
%error but also to its direction. Around a solved state $x^\star$,
%\begin{equation}
%    r(x^\star+e;u)
%    =
%    J(x^\star;u)e+\mathcal{O}(\|e\|^2),
%    \label{eq:local_residual}
%\end{equation}
%where $J$ is the AC residual Jacobian.
To relate this error decomposition to physical consistency, we use a
first-order Taylor expansion of the AC residual around a converged
power-flow solution $x^\star$:
\begin{equation}
    r(x^\star+e;u)
    =
    \underbrace{r(x^\star;u)}_{=0}
    +
    \underbrace{J(x^\star;u)e}_{\text{first-order term}}
    +
    \underbrace{\mathcal{O}(\|e\|^2)}_{\text{higher-order terms}},
    \label{eq:local_residual}
\end{equation}
where $J$ is the AC residual Jacobian.
Thus, the Jacobian determines how a given voltage error is translated
into power-balance residual: errors aligned with directions of larger
Jacobian gain can produce substantially larger residuals even when their
magnitudes are similar.
This motivates testing whether AC power balance changes when the voltage
error is rotated toward or away from the training-derived solution
subspace while its magnitude is held fixed.

\subsection{Calibrated solution-subspace projection}

This motivates a direct intervention: suppress prediction components
outside the dominant training-solution directions while preserving the
variation supported by the data. Because systematic bias and directional
error are distinct, we first apply the post-training calibration and then
project the calibrated prediction:
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    \hat x^{\mathrm{CSP}}
    =
    \bar x_{\mathrm{tr}}
    +
    P_k
    \left(
        \hat x-\hat b-\bar x_{\mathrm{tr}}
    \right)
    }
    \label{eq:csp}
\end{equation}
where $\hat b$ is estimated from the trained model's predictions on the
training set and frozen at test time, while $\bar x_{\mathrm{tr}}$ and
$P_k$ are obtained solely from the training reference solutions.
Calibration removes systematic output bias, whereas projection removes
components outside the dominant training-solution directions. The two
operations are complementary: projection alone can retain the component
of the bias lying inside the subspace, whereas CSP removes the bias before
restricting the remaining prediction.

CSP is a data-driven output correction rather than a projection onto the
nonlinear AC-feasible set, and therefore does not guarantee exact
satisfaction of the AC equations or operational constraints. Instead, it
provides a direct test of whether removing off-subspace prediction
components improves both voltage accuracy and recomputed AC power-balance
residuals. All calibration statistics, subspace bases, and rank-selection
criteria are determined without using test-set residuals.

\section{Experiments and Results}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}

Our primary case study is the 2224-bus GBnetwork with 37,022 converged
Newton--Raphson operating points, split approximately 1:1:1 into training,
validation, and test sets.
The data vary system-wide load/generation levels together with bus-level
$P/Q$ demand, generator active power, and PV voltage setpoints.
We evaluate PIGNN-GC, a global-context extension of
PIGNN-Attn-LS~\cite{kim2026pignn}, GridSFM~\cite{yang2026gridsfm},
GridFM-GraphKit~\cite{gridfmgraphkit2026}, and
LUMINA~\cite{li2026lumina}; cross-grid breadth is additionally evaluated
with GridSFM over 31 systems.

All calibration statistics and solution bases are fitted on training data
only. From $k\in\{4,8,16,32,64\}$, validation Mean PB selects
$k^\star=16$ without degrading calibrated voltage RMSE; this rank is then
frozen for all test and distribution-shift experiments.

Voltage RMSE alone can be small even when a model largely predicts a
static per-bus profile rather than following scenario-dependent voltage
variation~\cite{yang2026gridsfm}. To isolate this scenario-dependent
response, we center each bus as
$\tilde{V}_{is}=V_{is}-\bar{V}_i$ and
$\tilde{\hat{V}}_{is}=\hat{V}_{is}-\bar{\hat{V}}_i$, and report
\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{w}}^2
=
\operatorname{corr}^2
\left(\tilde{V}_{is},\tilde{\hat{V}}_{is}\right),
\qquad
a_{\mathrm{w}}
=
\frac{\operatorname{Cov}
\left(\tilde{V}_{is},\tilde{\hat{V}}_{is}\right)}
{\operatorname{Var}\left(\tilde{V}_{is}\right)} .
\end{equation}
$R_{\mathrm{w}}^2$ measures whether the model follows the
scenario-to-scenario voltage pattern, while $a_{\mathrm{w}}$ measures
how much of the true voltage variation it reproduces.
A per-bus constant predictor gives
$R_{\mathrm{w}}^2=a_{\mathrm{w}}=0$ under our convention,
while per-bus calibration leaves both metrics unchanged
because the offset vanishes after centering.

\subsection{GBnetwork: Error Direction and Calibrated Subspace Projection}
\label{sec:gb_mechanism}

\noindent\textit{Fixed-norm directional intervention.}
We vary the orientation of the voltage-magnitude error relative to the
training-derived solution subspace while holding its $\ell_2$ norm fixed,
thereby isolating direction from error magnitude.
Starting from the calibrated prediction, let
\begin{equation}
\small
\tilde e_{\perp}(\alpha)
=\|e_V\|_2
\frac{e_V^\parallel+\alpha e_V^\perp}
{\|e_V^\parallel+\alpha e_V^\perp\|_2},
\quad
\tilde e_{\parallel}(\alpha)
=\|e_V\|_2
\frac{\alpha e_V^\parallel+e_V^\perp}
{\|\alpha e_V^\parallel+e_V^\perp\|_2}.
\label{eq:controlled_geometry}
\end{equation}
By construction,
$\|\tilde e_{\perp}(\alpha)\|_2
=\|\tilde e_{\parallel}(\alpha)\|_2
=\|e_V\|_2$.
The calibrated phase angle is
held fixed, so this experiment changes only the direction of the
voltage-magnitude error. We reconstruct
\begin{equation}
\tilde{\underline{V}}(\alpha)
=
\left(V^\star+\tilde e(\alpha)\right)\odot e^{\jmath\hat\theta^C}
\end{equation}
and report
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Relative~PB}(\alpha)
=
\frac{\mathrm{Mean~PB}(\alpha)}
     {\mathrm{Mean~PB}(1)} .
\end{equation}

Figure~\ref{fig:error_geometry} directly confirms that residual magnitude depends on error
orientation. At $\alpha=0$, off-subspace suppression lowers Relative

(0.954 and 0.997), but the control intervention again moves PB upward
(1.349 and 1.206). Since the voltage-error norm is held fixed, the observed
changes isolate the effect of error direction on physical consistency.

Calibration substantially reduces voltage RMSE but leaves
$R_{\mathrm{w}}^2$ and $a_{\mathrm{w}}$ unchanged, as expected for a
per-bus translation. Projection acts on a different component: for

($R_{\mathrm{w}}^2=a_{\mathrm{w}}=0$) despite a moderate Mean PB.

\subsection{Cross-grid breadth and distribution shift}
\label{sec:breadth_shift}

\noindent\textit{Cross-grid breadth.}
Fig.~\ref{fig:crossgrid_csp} reports GridSFM Mean PB before and after CSP$_{16}$ on 31 systems.
The benchmark spans 4--9,241 buses and sub-kV to 750-kV voltage levels,
and consists of 28 pandapower/PYPOWER-format systems from IEEE, PEGASE,
RTE, and GB benchmark families, plus three Common Information Model
(CIM)-based network models; full details are provided in the project repository.
CSP$_{16}$ consistently reduces Mean PB across the full range
of network sizes, showing that its benefit is not confined to GBnetwork.

\noindent\textit{Topology and spatial shifts.}
We next keep a PIGNN-GC checkpoint and $k=16$ fixed while changing the
evaluation distribution. For $N$--1 and $N$--2, the control basis is
transferred without modification to unseen line-outage topologies. GBcorr
instead redistributes load across connected zones using a GB-derived spatial

Table~\ref{tab:shift} reveals two qualitatively different failure modes.
Under $N$--1 and $N$--2 outages, voltage-magnitude tracking remains close
to control ($R_{\mathrm{w}}^2\approx0.84$), and the bulk of the PB
distribution changes little: Mean PB rises from $0.0581$ to $0.0603$ and
$0.0617$, while p99 moves only from $0.5462$ to $0.5632$ and $0.5721$.
The maxima, however, increase sharply to $33.16$ and $27.45$,
while the bulk statistics remain close to control, indicating that
topology shift primarily introduces isolated extreme violations.

GBcorr produces a broad rather than tail-dominated shift.
$R_{\mathrm{w}}^2$ drops from $0.8476$ to $0.4819$, while Mean PB rises
to $0.1003$ and p99 to $0.9746$; the median and p95 increase as well.
Condition-matched recalibration and subspace refitting reduces
Mean PB to $0.0680$ and p99 to $0.6415$, while improving $|V|$ RMSE.
Tracking recovers only partially ($R_{\mathrm{w}}^2=0.5150$),
indicating that basis adaptation can improve physical consistency,
whereas recovering scenario-dependent tracking under this shift may
require updating the surrogate parameters using data from the shifted
operating distribution.

\section{CONCLUSION}

This work advances the understanding of neural AC power-flow surrogates by
identifying output-error geometry as a key source of the gap between
voltage accuracy and physical consistency. Across physics-informed and
foundation-model approaches, we show that small voltage errors can contain
off-subspace components that induce large AC power-balance violations. Building on this insight,
calibrated solution-subspace projection removes
off-subspace prediction components, reducing both voltage error and AC
power-balance residuals across diverse grids.
These results motivate extending physics-consistent neural surrogates toward
broader power-system analysis, estimation, and optimization.
